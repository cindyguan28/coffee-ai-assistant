# How to Keep a Coffee Journal You’ll Actually Use

You remember that a coffee was good. You may even remember the café or the color of the bag. But a few weeks later, the useful details are gone: Was it the washed Ethiopian coffee or the natural one? Did the finer grind make it sweeter, or was that the cup that turned bitter? Would you buy it again?

A coffee journal gives those answers somewhere to live.

It does not need to be a professional cupping form or a spreadsheet full of measurements. The best coffee journal is the one you can update quickly and understand when you return to it. Start with the coffee itself, record how much you enjoyed it, and add brewing detail only when that detail will help you next time.

## What is a coffee journal?

A coffee journal is a personal record of the coffees you drink and the experiences you have with them. It can help you:

- remember beans and roasters you enjoyed;
- repeat a brew that worked;
- avoid buying a coffee you did not enjoy;
- notice patterns in origins, roast levels, processes, and flavors;
- describe your own taste with more confidence.

A tasting journal focuses mainly on what a coffee tasted and felt like. A brew log focuses on the recipe and variables used to prepare it. A useful personal coffee journal can connect both without forcing you to complete every field for every cup.

## Start with the Bean, not the form

When you open a new bag, begin with the details that identify it:

- coffee name;
- roaster;
- country or region;
- roast date, if available;
- roast level;
- process, such as washed or natural;
- tasting notes printed on the bag.

You do not need to research missing information before saving the coffee. If all you know is the name and roaster, start there. A journal should reduce the effort of remembering, not create homework before you can drink the coffee.

Keeping the roaster’s tasting notes is still useful even if you cannot taste every listed flavor. They describe how the coffee was presented and give you language to revisit after a few brews. Your own experience can be recorded separately.

## Record the first Brew that teaches you something

You do not have to log every cup. Record a brew when you are trying a coffee for the first time, changing a variable, or getting a result you want to repeat.

For a simple brew log, record:

- brew method;
- coffee dose;
- water or beverage yield;
- grind setting;
- brew time;
- water temperature, when it matters to your method;
- one sentence about the result;
- what you would change next time.

If you use a V60—a cone-shaped pour-over brewer—an entry might look like this:

> **Coffee:** Washed coffee from Ethiopia, roasted for filter  
> **Method:** V60 pour-over  
> **Recipe:** 15 g coffee · 250 g water · 2 minutes 45 seconds  
> **Taste:** Bright and floral, but slightly thin  
> **Next time:** Keep the grind setting and try a little less water

Writing the fields out once makes the entry understandable even to someone who has never used brew-ratio shorthand. After the format becomes familiar, the same information can be shortened to `15 g / 250 g · 2:45` without losing its meaning.

For a quicker visual summary, the same entry can be reduced to four lines:

> **Coffee** · Washed Ethiopia  
> **Brew** · V60 · 15 g / 250 g · 2:45  
> **Result** · Floral and bright, slightly thin  
> **Next** · Use a little less water

This `Coffee → Brew → Result → Next` pattern is fast to scan while still showing what each number and observation belongs to.

Here is the same pattern for an espresso setup:

> **Coffee** · Bellissimo Italian Blend · espresso blend  
> **Machine** · Isomac espresso machine  
> **Grind** · Setting 8 on my grinder  
> **Brew** · 18 g in · 36 g out · 28 seconds  
> **Result** · Smooth and chocolatey, but slightly bitter at the end  
> **Next** · Keep the dose and yield; try one small step coarser

This is an illustrative journal entry, not a universal recipe. A grind-setting number is meaningful only when it is connected to the same grinder: `8` on one grinder may be completely different from `8` on another. Recording the grinder model—or saving it once as part of your setup—makes the setting useful when you return to the coffee later.

That final sentence is often more useful than another number. It turns a record into a decision for the next brew.

If you drink espresso, grind setting, dose, yield, and extraction time may be central to reproducing the cup. If you usually drink café coffee, the café, drink, bean, and your reaction may be enough. The journal should match the way you actually drink coffee.

## Use ordinary words for Taste

You do not need to identify lychee, bergamot, or cacao nibs to keep a worthwhile tasting journal. Begin with questions you can answer honestly:

- Did I enjoy it?
- Did it feel bright or mellow?
- Was the bitterness pleasant or distracting?
- Did it feel light, creamy, or heavy?
- Was there a flavor I would recognize again?
- Would I buy or brew this coffee again?

“Sweet, clean, and easy to drink” is a useful tasting note. So is “too bitter after it cooled.” Personal notes are not an exam, and they do not need to agree with the words printed on the bag.

When you want more structure, you can rate a few consistent dimensions such as acidity, sweetness, bitterness, body, balance, and aroma. Consistency matters more than precision. Your scores are most valuable when they help you compare your own coffees over time.

## Separate liking from tasting

A strong coffee is not automatically a coffee you dislike. A highly acidic coffee is not automatically a good one. Sensory intensity and personal enjoyment answer different questions.

Try keeping one simple liking score alongside any tasting dimensions:

- **Liking:** How happy were you with this cup?
- **Acidity:** How noticeable was the bright or tangy quality?
- **Bitterness:** How strong was the bitter taste?
- **Body:** How light or heavy did the coffee feel?

This distinction makes your journal more personal. Over time, you may discover that you enjoy high aroma and sweetness but prefer a lighter body, or that you like natural-process coffees only with certain brew methods. Those patterns are more useful than a generic idea of what coffee is supposed to taste like.

## Review patterns, not isolated scores

The value of a coffee journal appears when you look back.

After five or ten coffees, ask:

- Which coffees received my highest liking scores?
- Which origins or processes appear repeatedly?
- Which flavor families show up in coffees I enjoy?
- Which brew method gives me the most consistent results?
- What adjustment most often fixes a disappointing cup?
- Which coffee would I happily buy again?

One entry tells you what happened today. A collection of entries begins to describe your taste.

## Keep the habit deliberately small

If journaling starts to feel like filling out a laboratory report, reduce it.

Use this minimum entry:

1. Which coffee was it?
2. How did you brew or order it?
3. Did you like it?
4. What would you remember or change?

Everything else is optional. You can always add more detail when comparing recipes or dialing in a difficult coffee.

It also helps to keep the journal close to the moment. Add the bean when you open the bag. Add the brew while the coffee cools or immediately after drinking it. A short note captured now is more useful than a perfect note you never write.

## Paper, spreadsheet, or coffee journal app?

Paper is pleasant and distraction-free, but it becomes harder to search and compare as the journal grows. A spreadsheet is flexible, especially for detailed recipes, but may feel like maintenance rather than a coffee ritual.

A dedicated coffee journal is most useful when it connects the same bean to multiple brews and gradually turns your history into something you can understand. Whichever format you choose, make sure you can quickly answer two questions:

> Have I tried this coffee before?  
> What should I repeat or change next time?

## Begin with the coffee on your shelf

You do not need to reconstruct every coffee you have ever tasted. Start with the bag you are drinking now.

Save its name. Log one brew. Write one honest sentence. That is enough to begin building a useful record of your coffee life—and, eventually, a clearer picture of the coffee you genuinely love.

## Trusted coffee references

You do not need professional terminology to keep a personal journal. When you want a more consistent vocabulary, these independent industry resources are useful starting points:

- The [World Coffee Research Sensory Lexicon](https://worldcoffeeresearch.org/resources/sensory-lexicon) defines 110 flavor, aroma, and texture attributes and includes sensory references for comparing intensity.
- The [Specialty Coffee Association Coffee Taster’s Flavor Wheel](https://sca.coffee/store) organizes a shared vocabulary for describing coffee and was updated in collaboration with World Coffee Research.
- The [SCA Coffee Value Assessment](https://sca.coffee/value-assessment/) separates descriptive sensory information from broader value assessment, a useful reminder that describing a coffee and deciding whether you personally like it are not the same task.

These are professional reference systems, not rules that a home coffee journal must copy. Use them when they help you find a word; keep your own liking and next-brew decision at the center of the entry.

## Proposed FAQ block

### What should I write in a coffee journal?

Start with the coffee name, roaster, brew method, whether you liked it, and one sentence about what you would remember or change. Add recipe variables only when they help you compare or repeat a brew.

### Do I need to understand professional coffee tasting?

No. Ordinary descriptions such as bright, sweet, bitter, light, creamy, or balanced are useful. Your journal is meant to describe your experience, not test your vocabulary.

### Should I record every cup of coffee?

Not necessarily. Record first attempts, meaningful changes, excellent cups, and disappointing cups you want to learn from. A smaller journal you maintain is more useful than a detailed one you abandon.

### What is the difference between a coffee journal and a brew log?

A brew log focuses on preparation variables and results. A coffee journal can also remember the bean, roaster, origin, tasting experience, and personal preferences across many brews.
